# Thinking Methods App: Copyright-Safer Product Guidance

> **Scope:** Product-design and content guidance, not legal advice. This document addresses copyright at the level of general workflows and presentation. It does not clear a finished implementation, trademark use, patent issues, contracts, or laws outside the applicable jurisdiction.

## Executive conclusion

Your proposed application workflows do **not** need to change solely for copyright reasons at this level of detail. Features such as private submissions, simultaneous reveal, scoring, discussion, ranking, and assigning actions describe functional processes rather than protected expression.

Keep all six thinking methods. Build an original presentation around them: write your own instructional copy, prompts, examples, diagrams, templates, UI, and code. A final legal review should focus on the exact content, assets, branding, launch markets, and any third-party materials included at release.

## Important distinctions

### Names of methods

Under U.S. copyright law, names and short titles generally are not copyright-protected. You do not need to rename these methods solely to avoid copyright concerns.

- Use descriptive names such as **Five Whys**, **Decision Matrix**, or **Fishbone Analysis** where they accurately identify a method.
- Avoid implying sponsorship, certification, partnership, endorsement, or ownership by another organization.

Further reading: [U.S. Copyright Office — Copyright Protection Not Available for Names, Titles, or Short Phrases](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf)

### Attribution is not permission

Linking to or crediting a source can be useful for users who want background reading. However, attribution does not substitute for permission when you reproduce protected text, illustrations, templates, videos, or other original material.

- Cite sources as optional learning resources.
- Do not copy their teaching text, example scenarios, worksheets, diagrams, screenshots, downloadable files, or code unless the terms clearly allow it or you have permission.

Further reading: [Federal copyright FAQ](https://www.cendi.gov/pdf/FAQ_Copyright_30jan18.pdf)

### Branding and trademarks

Using a generic feature name such as “Decision Matrix” differs from claiming that a feature is “ASQ-certified,” displaying an organization’s logo, or otherwise suggesting affiliation. Those can create trademark or false-affiliation issues separate from copyright.

- Do not use third-party logos without permission.
- Do not claim certification, endorsement, partnership, or official status unless it is true and authorized.
- If a public-facing product will use third-party brands prominently, conduct trademark clearance before launch.

Further reading: [USPTO — Likelihood of Confusion](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/search/likelihood-confusion)

## Build-original rule

For every method, independently create the following:

- Product copy and method introductions
- Step-by-step facilitator instructions
- Prompts and tooltips
- Worked examples and fictional scenarios
- Diagrams, illustrations, icons, and visual layouts
- Templates, worksheets, tables, and downloadable exports
- UI components, interaction design, and source code
- AI-generated summaries or coaching prompts, reviewed for originality and accuracy

Avoid importing or closely paraphrasing a source’s distinctive explanation, visual example, downloadable worksheet, or teaching sequence. The underlying method may be usable; a particular author’s expression of it may not be.

## Product implementation suggestions

### 1. Use original microcopy

Write in your own product voice. For example, instead of importing a published definition of the Five Whys, use original language such as:

> Start with the outcome you want to understand. Ask what directly contributed to it, then ask what contributed to that answer. Continue until the group reaches a cause it can investigate or act on.

This communicates the procedure without reproducing a source’s expression.

### 2. Make diagrams programmatic and distinct

For a fishbone view, generate branches from the user’s own categories and inputs. Use your own spacing, colors, typography, labels, and interactions rather than recreating a particular online illustration or slide.

### 3. Treat examples as product assets

Examples are often where copying occurs. Create fictional, original examples, such as:

- A creator deciding which content series to launch
- A school team investigating delayed student feedback
- A small software team choosing a feature for its next release
- A marketing group testing whether a new landing-page headline improves signups

Do not reproduce a publisher’s scenario, data, visual treatment, or answer sequence too closely.

### 4. Add useful provenance without overclaiming

An optional “Learn more” panel may link to reputable historical or educational sources. Frame links as background reading, not as evidence that the source endorses your product.

Suggested wording:

> This tool uses a widely known problem-solving method. Explore the links below for independent background reading.

### 5. Include a neutral product disclaimer

A concise user-facing disclaimer can help set expectations:

> These tools support structured discussion and decision-making. They do not replace professional, legal, medical, financial, scientific, or safety advice where specialized judgment is required.

## Pre-launch checklist

Before releasing the app, verify that:

- Every paragraph of instructional copy was written for your product or properly licensed.
- Every example, worksheet, image, icon, video, and diagram is original, licensed, public domain, or used under a valid exception.
- No screenshots, diagrams, or downloadable templates were imported from third-party instructional sites without reuse permission.
- Brand names, logos, certifications, and affiliation claims have been reviewed separately for trademark risk.
- Open-source code, fonts, UI kits, icon sets, and AI models comply with their licenses and attribution conditions.
- AI-generated assets have been reviewed for unwanted similarity to recognizable third-party content.
- Terms of service, privacy practices, and user-content rules cover private submissions, simultaneous reveal, retention, moderation, and exports.
- A qualified intellectual-property lawyer reviews the final product where the commercial stakes justify it.

## Bottom line

No method license appears necessary on copyright grounds for the functionality described here. Keep the six methods and implement them through your own expression. The relevant risk is not the general technique; it is copying specific protected content or creating misleading brand affiliation. Final clearance depends on the exact implementation, assets, jurisdictions, and launch plan.
